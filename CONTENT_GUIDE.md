# AI Adventure Lab Content Guide

This guide keeps new content aligned with the current playable paths.

## Elementary Student Standard: Grades 3-5

Elementary content should be concrete, encouraging, and easy to understand without turning each round into a reading test. Situations should stay close to school, family, friendship, creativity, games, and everyday safety.

Good scenario areas:

- animal reports, book responses, math practice, vocabulary, science posters, and story planning
- simple factual claims with one clear detail to check
- prompts that name the topic, goal, amount, reading level, or desired format
- getting hints, examples, questions, practice, and feedback without copying finished work
- usernames, passwords, school names, addresses, private messages, photos, locations, and routines
- moments where a parent, guardian, teacher, school nurse, or other trusted adult should help

Avoid:

- long answer choices, abstract research terms, citation rules, or adult situations
- asking children to judge whether writing was secretly produced by AI
- scary scam, health, or safety scenarios with unnecessary detail
- prompts that normalize sharing names, schools, addresses, schedules, passwords, or private messages
- presenting AI as a person that knows feelings, keeps secrets, or makes real-world decisions

Core habits to reinforce:

- introduce AI with guided examples before asking a first-time child to complete scored judgment questions
- provide a no-penalty guided first round and optional hints as children learn each new interaction
- give a trusted clue when checking facts so the activity measures comparison and verification instead of uncommon background knowledge
- AI can suggest ideas and explain concepts, but people make decisions and handle real-world safety
- check an exact fact in a trusted book, classroom resource, official page, or with a trusted adult
- ask clear questions that include the goal and the kind of help needed
- use AI for learning support while keeping thinking, creativity, and final schoolwork with the student
- use placeholders and general descriptions instead of identifying or private information
- ask a trusted adult when a question involves health, safety, location, rules, or another person's wellbeing

## High School Student Standard

High School content should feel realistic for grades 9-12. Scenarios should be clear, practical, and age-appropriate without becoming elementary or overly adult.

Good scenario areas:

- essays, projects, presentations, and class discussions
- tutoring, studying, test prep, and learning without cheating
- school AI-use rules, rubrics, and teacher expectations
- clubs, sports, volunteering, scholarships, college/career prep, and part-time jobs
- group chats, social media, screenshots, private messages, and classmate privacy
- current events, science claims, statistics, quotes, and source checking
- bias in recommendations, clubs, hiring, discipline, scholarships, access, and visibility

Avoid:

- content that feels too young for high school
- adult-only legal, workplace, finance, or parenting scenarios
- college-level research complexity unless simplified
- medical, legal, or financial advice that sounds definitive
- prompts that encourage cheating, copying final answers, or bypassing teachers

Core habits to reinforce:

- ask for the assignment rules, rubric, goal, and what the student already tried
- when teaching student prompt clarity, show the weak first prompt and ask players to choose the stronger replacement prompt
- use AI for explanation, feedback, practice, and planning instead of final answers to copy
- check source title, author or organization, date, publication, link/DOI, and what the source actually supports
- summarize or anonymize private details before asking AI for help
- watch for confident answers that do not provide evidence
- choose fair criteria instead of shortcuts like confidence, popularity, device access, or background

## Middle School Student Standard

Middle School content should feel realistic for grades 6-8. Scenarios should be concrete, short, and practical. The player should usually understand the situation without knowing advanced research terms or high school planning tasks.

Good scenario areas:

- homework help, study practice, vocabulary, class presentations, and group projects
- simple fact checks, famous-name traps, quotes, weather, school rumors, and student-friendly sources
- friend privacy, screenshots, group chats, usernames, passwords, school names, addresses, routes, and routines
- fairness in clubs, teams, recommendations, classroom help, and access to technology
- moments where a student should ask a teacher, parent, guardian, coach, nurse, or trusted adult before acting

Avoid:

- college prep, scholarships, job-search tasks, or complex citation standards
- adult-only finance, workplace, legal, medical, or parenting scenarios
- long answer choices that make the round feel like a reading test
- prompts that ask AI to complete schoolwork, hide AI use, share private information, or bypass adults

Core habits to reinforce:

- use AI for hints, examples, practice, outlines, and feedback instead of finished answers to copy
- replace vague first prompts with clearer prompts that include class topic, goal, context, and useful format
- check whether a source names the organization, title, topic, and what claim it supports
- remove names, screenshots, addresses, routines, passwords, and login codes from prompts
- choose fair criteria instead of stereotypes, popularity, personality shortcuts, or device access
- pause and ask a trusted adult when AI advice involves health, safety, downloads, school rules, or online trends

## College / Adult Learner Standard

College content should feel useful for students who are more independent but still working inside academic rules, campus policies, and real-world consequences.

Good scenario areas:

- literature reviews, research papers, abstracts, discussion posts, lab reports, and draft feedback
- professor instructions, syllabus rules, citation style, office hours, and academic integrity boundaries
- library databases, Google Scholar, DOI-style details, report titles, methods, samples, and source limitations
- internships, resumes, cover letters, campus jobs, student organizations, and leadership roles
- roommate issues, campus health, financial aid, background checks, identity data, and campus policies
- bias in student leadership, group work, hiring, admissions, event feedback, resource access, and prestige shortcuts

Avoid:

- treating AI as a source without verification
- having AI write final academic submissions
- fake citations that look too usable without clearly teaching verification
- advice that replaces professors, librarians, medical professionals, financial aid offices, or official policy
- oversharing student IDs, SSNs, financial aid details, medical records, room numbers, or private conflicts

Core habits to reinforce:

- use AI as a research assistant, writing coach, study partner, or planning helper
- verify every source through library databases, Google Scholar, official reports, or course materials
- check whether a source actually supports the claim, not just whether it sounds academic
- separate what AI knows, what it inferred, and what still needs verification
- provide assignment context, source boundaries, rubric criteria, and academic integrity limits
- remove private campus, health, finance, identity, and roommate details before asking for help
- choose fair criteria instead of popularity, prestige, stereotypes, convenience, or majority-only summaries

## Everyday User Standard

Everyday User content should focus on practical, common situations where AI can help but should not replace verification or judgment.

Current playable categories:

- Claim Check
- AI Reality Check
- Scams and Suspicious Messages
- Better Everyday Prompts
- Follow-Up Coach

Core habits to reinforce:

- pause before trusting, sharing, clicking, or replying
- verify through original sources or official channels
- check dates, context, author/organization, source quality, and what the source actually supports
- watch for pressure, urgency, suspicious links, requests for codes, and requests for payment
- avoid sharing private details unless they are necessary and safe
- give AI enough goal, context, constraints, and output format to be useful
- ask follow-up questions that clarify, prioritize, verify, or personalize the first answer
- for prompt-improvement rounds, show the weak initial prompt, the weak AI response, and answer choices that are actual improved prompts

Possible future Everyday User categories, after playtesting current levels:

- Health Caution
- Shopping Research
- Family And Personal Privacy
- Everyday Finance Caution
- Travel And Local Planning
